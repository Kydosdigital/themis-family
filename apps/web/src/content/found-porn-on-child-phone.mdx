Maybe you found it in the browser history.

Maybe another child sent it.

Maybe you had already put filters in place and now you have discovered a private browser, a different site or a way around them.

For a lot of parents, the first emotion is shock.

Then the questions start.

*How long has this been happening? What have they seen? Is this normal curiosity? Are they addicted? Has this changed how they think about sex? Have I completely missed something?*

Before you turn the discovery into an interrogation, there is one important distinction to make:

**What you found tells you that something happened. It does not yet tell you why.**

## Do not diagnose the situation from one browser tab

Children and teenagers can encounter explicit content in very different ways.

The [NSPCC's current parent guidance](https://www.nspcc.org.uk/keeping-children-safe/online-safety/inappropriate-explicit-content/online-porn/) lists reasons including curiosity, trying to learn about sex or sexual identity, sexual arousal, jokes, rule-breaking, peer or relationship pressure, and accidental exposure through misleading links or pop-ups.

Those situations do not all call for exactly the same response.

A 2024 [systematic review of factors associated with adolescents' use of sexually explicit internet material](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39424699/) also found substantial variation between studies, including differences in how use was defined and measured.

So resist the urge to decide what this means before you have spoken to your child.

## Before the conversation, ask yourself four questions

### 1. What do I actually know?

Did you see one search, repeated history, saved content, a private browser, messages from another person, or something else?

Be precise.

### 2. Is there an immediate safety concern?

This matters more than punishment.

Look for signs that somebody else may be involved:

- an adult or unknown person sending sexual material
- requests for nude or sexual images
- threats or blackmail
- pressure from a boyfriend, girlfriend or peer
- sexualised conversations that appear coercive
- attempts to arrange unsafe meetings
- child sexual abuse material

If another person is targeting, coercing or threatening your child, treat that as a safeguarding issue, not simply a pornography issue.

### 3. How old is my child?

The same discovery means something very different with an eight-year-old and a sixteen-year-old.

Younger children generally need more direct protection and simpler language.

Older teenagers still need boundaries and guidance, but the conversation increasingly has to include sexuality, consent, privacy, relationships and independent judgement.

### 4. Am I calm enough to have the first conversation?

You do not have to be perfectly calm.

You just need to be calm enough that your child has a chance of telling you the truth.

## What I would say first

Something like:

> "I found sexual content on your phone. I am not starting this conversation to embarrass you. I need to understand what happened, what you have been seeing, and whether anyone else is involved."

Then stop talking for a moment.

Give them a chance.

Useful questions include:

- Did you search for it or did somebody send it?
- Is this the first time?
- Have you seen anything that upset or frightened you?
- Has anybody asked you to send something back?
- Has anybody pressured you to keep a conversation secret?
- Is there anything you are worried I will find out?
- What do you think porn gets wrong about real sex and relationships?

The last question can be especially useful with teenagers because it moves the conversation beyond "caught versus not caught".

## Do not make shame the teaching method

You may strongly dislike what you found.

You can say so.

You can maintain a rule that your child is not allowed to access pornography.

You can change device settings and increase supervision.

None of that requires humiliating your child.

Shame makes it harder for a child to come back if the next situation is more serious.

That matters because explicit content can overlap with sexting, pressure, coercion and image-based abuse. A child who believes "I will be destroyed if Mum or Dad finds out" has one more reason to hide when they actually need help.

## Talk about what pornography is teaching

"Do not watch it" is a boundary.

It is not a complete education.

Older children and teenagers may also need help understanding that pornography can give distorted impressions of:

- bodies
- sexual performance
- consent
- pleasure
- gender roles
- what partners are expected to do
- what healthy communication looks like

The NSPCC specifically recommends open conversations about the risks, alongside technical controls.

This is one of the places where parents sometimes feel awkward because the topic becomes bigger than the phone.

That is exactly why the conversation matters.

If porn becomes the child's main source of sex education, silence from adults is still teaching something.

## Should I put stronger parental controls on?

Often, yes, particularly for younger children or where a control has clearly failed.

The NSPCC recommends parental controls and child-friendly settings as one layer of prevention.

But be clear about what the control is for.

A filter can reduce access.

It cannot answer the questions your child already has.

It cannot teach consent.

It cannot tell them what to do when a friend sends explicit material.

It cannot guarantee they will never see pornography on another person's device.

Use the boundary, then teach what the boundary is trying to protect.

## "Is my child addicted?"

Do not make that diagnosis from a single discovery, or even from the fact that the content has been viewed repeatedly.

Researchers themselves use different definitions and measures when studying adolescent pornography use.

A [2025 rapid systematic review of longitudinal studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40631652/) distinguishes pornography use from problematic pornography use. A [2026 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41081900/) also examined associations between online pornography use and psychological symptoms, but an association is not the same thing as proving that pornography caused a particular problem in an individual child.

Instead of reaching for a label, look at functioning and distress.

Questions worth asking include:

- Does your child feel unable to stop even when they want to?
- Is the behaviour becoming more frequent or more extreme?
- Is it significantly affecting sleep, school, relationships or everyday responsibilities?
- Is your child distressed by their own use?
- Is it linked with other serious emotional or behavioural concerns?

If you are worried that the behaviour is compulsive or is significantly affecting your child, speak with an appropriately qualified health or mental-health professional.

## If the content was sent by somebody else

This changes the conversation.

Ask who sent it, whether your child knows them, whether the sender has requested anything in return, and whether threats or pressure are involved.

If an adult is sexualising contact with a child, if somebody is blackmailing your child, or if intimate images of a minor are being distributed, seek appropriate safeguarding help for your country.

In the UK, parents can use the [NSPCC online safety guidance](https://www.nspcc.org.uk/keeping-children-safe/online-safety/) and contact the NSPCC Helpline. Children can contact Childline. If a child is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

Do not ask your child to conduct their own investigation or continue a dangerous conversation just to collect evidence.

## What if they bypassed a filter?

Then you have two conversations.

One is about explicit content.

The other is about deliberately bypassing a family boundary.

Do not muddle them together so completely that you learn nothing about either.

You might say:

> "We will deal with the fact that you got around the restriction, because that broke an agreement. I also need to understand the content itself and whether anything unsafe is happening. Those are two separate things."

That clarity can lower the temperature.

## What should change after the conversation?

The answer depends on age and what you discovered, but the plan may include:

- repairing or strengthening filters
- limiting installation of alternative browsers
- moving devices out of bedrooms at night
- reviewing which devices are included in family controls
- agreeing what happens if sexual content appears accidentally
- discussing consent and healthy relationships
- agreeing that the child can ask questions without being mocked
- setting a follow-up conversation rather than pretending one awkward talk solved everything

For an older teenager, privacy should also be part of the discussion.

If you are increasing monitoring because of a specific concern, explain why and explain what would need to happen for that supervision to reduce again.

## The goal is not a child who never asks sexual questions

Curiosity is not the enemy.

The long-term goal is a young person who can:

- recognise unrealistic or harmful sexual messages
- understand consent
- resist pressure
- ask for help
- make safer choices
- know that an adult can handle the truth if something goes wrong

That is why Themis does not believe the answer to digital parenting is simply "block more".

Sometimes blocking is exactly what protection requires.

But the boundary should create space for the conversation and the learning that have to come next.
