You say the game is finished.

Your child says, "One minute."

Five minutes later, you say it again.

Now they are angry. Maybe shouting. Maybe bargaining. Maybe suddenly you are the worst parent in the world because you asked for the controller.

It is easy to look at that reaction and think:

**This has to be addiction.**

Sometimes gaming can become genuinely problematic.

But anger at the end of a game does not diagnose it.

## Stopping a game can be a difficult transition

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that even teenagers can become angry, sullen or defiant when they are asked to step away from digital media.

There are several reasons the transition can be hard.

The game may be highly rewarding.

Your child may be in the middle of a match that cannot be paused.

Their friends may be relying on them.

They may be close to completing something they have worked toward for days.

They may also simply be tired, hungry or stressed and using the game as a way to switch off.

None of those explanations mean the boundary disappears.

They help you understand the moment you are trying to manage.

## Do not wait until the final second to decide the stopping rule

A lot of gaming conflict begins before the game starts.

If the agreement is:

> "You can play for a bit."

then parent and child may be imagining completely different endings.

Make the stopping point visible beforehand.

For example:

> "You have 45 minutes. At 7:45, you finish the match you're in and then stop. Don't start a new one after 7:40."

Or:

> "You can play until dinner. I'll give you a 15-minute warning and a five-minute warning."

The exact rule will depend on the game.

If you know that a match normally lasts 25 minutes, a rule that demands immediate shutdown at an arbitrary second may create avoidable conflict.

You are still setting the limit.

You are just setting one that understands the activity.

## Warnings work differently for different children

Some children do better with:

- a 15-minute warning
- a five-minute warning
- a timer they set themselves
- a console limit
- a visible clock
- a "last match" rule

Others hear repeated warnings as repeated interruptions and become more frustrated.

Experiment.

The goal is not to find the parenting technique that makes your child delighted to stop having fun.

The goal is to make the transition predictable enough that everyone knows what happens next.

## Hold the boundary without turning the moment into a lecture

The middle of a meltdown is a poor time for a speech about dopamine, school performance or gratitude.

The AAP's [guidance on screen-time tantrums](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/screen-time-and-temper-tantrums-helpful-tips-for-parents.aspx) advises staying calm, using few words and avoiding a long lecture in the moment.

Something simple is enough:

> "I know you're frustrated. The gaming time is finished. We can talk about what went wrong when you're calm."

Then talk later.

## Ask what exactly made stopping hard

When things have settled, be curious.

Was the problem:

- the amount of time?
- being interrupted in a match?
- letting teammates down?
- losing just before the end?
- wanting to avoid homework?
- not knowing what to do after gaming?
- feeling that siblings get more?
- the limit changing unexpectedly?

A child can be wrong to shout at you and still have useful information about the rule.

Understanding the trigger helps you design a better boundary next time.

## When should I worry about gaming itself?

The World Health Organization defines [gaming disorder](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/addictive-behaviours-gaming-disorder) around three broad features:

- impaired control over gaming
- gaming increasingly taking priority over other activities
- continuing or escalating gaming despite negative consequences

For diagnosis, the pattern must be serious enough to cause significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational or other important areas of functioning. The WHO notes that the pattern would normally be evident for at least 12 months, although severity can sometimes justify a shorter period.

That is very different from:

"My child complained loudly when Fortnite went off."

The [AAP's guidance on unhealthy gaming](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Unhealthy-Video-Gaming.aspx) similarly suggests looking at whether gaming is replacing homework, sleep, exercise or relationships, whether the child can cut down, and whether other interests are disappearing.

## Look at the whole week, not only the worst five minutes

Ask:

**Sleep:** Are they getting enough?

**School:** Is work being done?

**Movement:** Is gaming replacing virtually all physical activity?

**Relationships:** Are offline relationships disappearing?

**Other interests:** Is gaming the only thing they still enjoy?

**Control:** Can they stop sometimes, or is every limit impossible?

**Consequences:** Are serious problems continuing even though the child can see the damage?

A passionate gamer can still have a balanced life.

A child who plays fewer hours may still be using gaming in a way that is causing significant problems.

## Family connection matters too

A [2025 systematic review of 64 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40154103/) found parental knowledge and positive parenting among the protective family factors associated with lower gaming-disorder risk. Findings around restrictive mediation were inconclusive.

That does not mean limits are useless.

It means "make the limit stricter" is not the whole prevention strategy.

Know what your child plays.

Ask who they play with.

Let them show you something they are proud of.

Try the game if you can tolerate being terrible at it.

Understanding the hobby gives you information that a timer cannot.

## Consequences should be about the agreement

If your child repeatedly ignores the stopping rule, there can be a consequence.

Keep it connected.

For example:

- less flexible gaming time the next day
- gaming only in a shared area for a period
- no starting matches close to the cutoff
- a shorter session until stopping becomes reliable
- requiring the child to set the timer themselves

Avoid turning one angry shutdown into a punishment that removes every form of technology for weeks.

That often creates a bigger power struggle without teaching the stopping skill.

## Teach the skill underneath the rule

Eventually, there will be no parent standing beside the console.

The useful skills are:

- estimating how long an activity will take
- stopping before an obligation
- tolerating disappointment
- leaving a game even when friends stay online
- noticing when gaming is being used to avoid something
- protecting sleep
- handling frustration without aggression

That is why a good gaming boundary is more than a shutdown command.

It is practice.

## When to get extra help

Speak with an appropriately qualified professional if gaming is connected with significant and persistent problems such as:

- severe deterioration in sleep
- school refusal or major academic decline
- loss of important relationships
- aggression that creates a safety risk
- inability to reduce gaming despite wanting to
- serious distress when not gaming
- gaming becoming almost the only meaningful activity

Do not wait for the word "addiction" to become certain before asking for help.

You can ask for support because the family is struggling.
