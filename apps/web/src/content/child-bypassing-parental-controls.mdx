You set the limit. They found another browser.

You blocked that route. They switched devices.

At some point, it stops feeling like a settings problem and starts feeling personal. You agreed a rule, your child knew the rule, and they deliberately found a way around it.

That frustration is real. But before you decide what the workaround says about your child, separate two things:

**The parental control failed technically.**

**Your child wanted to get around the rule.**

You need to deal with both, but they are not the same problem.

## First, close the technical gap

If a restriction is supposed to be active and it is not, fix that first.

Check whether the child can reach the same service through:

- a web browser instead of the app
- another device
- a second account
- a private or incognito browser
- a web version of a blocked service
- a device that is not included in the family controls

This is not about trying to outsmart your child forever. A boundary that exists only in theory is confusing for everyone.

If the rule is "no YouTube after 9 PM", the family should not have to discover that youtube.com still works in a browser after the app has been restricted.

Once the technical boundary works the way you intended, there is still another question.

## Why did they want around it?

This is the point where families often jump straight from a workaround to a judgement:

"They are addicted."

"They have no respect for our rules."

"They cannot be trusted."

Sometimes the behaviour is serious. Sometimes it is repeated. Sometimes there is a much bigger problem underneath it.

But a workaround on its own does not tell you which of those things is true.

The reason could be boredom. It could be a group chat that suddenly became active. It could be a game event. It could be the feeling that everybody else is still online. It could be a rule that made sense six months ago but now feels unreasonable to an older child. It could also be simple boundary-testing.

Understanding the reason does not mean accepting the behaviour.

It tells you which problem you are actually trying to solve.

## What the research does, and does not, say

There is no good evidence for one simple rule such as "more monitoring always works" or "restrictions never work".

A [2026 systematic review of 27 studies involving 26,337 adolescents](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41069112/) identified several relevant parental factors, including modelling, communication, monitoring and limit-setting. The findings around monitoring were mixed. The review also found differences between proactive limit-setting and reactive limits introduced after problems had already developed.

A separate [2022 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35933287/) found that the overall associations between problematic internet use and media-specific active or restrictive mediation were weak or non-significant, and the authors cautioned against treating restriction as a complete answer, particularly with older adolescents.

That does **not** mean limits are pointless.

A limit can reduce access. It can create sleep space. It can stop a younger child reaching something they are not ready for. It can help a family keep a routine consistent.

What it cannot do by itself is teach the judgement your child will eventually need when you are no longer holding the settings password.

That is the part parents have to build alongside the control.

## What I would say tonight

You do not need a courtroom speech.

Try something closer to:

> "I know you found a way around the limit. We are going to fix that part, because the rule still applies. But I also want to understand why you wanted around it. Was it the amount of time, what you were doing, or something else?"

Then listen.

You may still disagree with the answer. That is fine.

The important thing is that the first conversation gives you information instead of simply producing a confession.

Useful follow-up questions are:

- What were you trying to do?
- Did you know the rule still applied even though the app let you in another way?
- What feels unfair about the rule?
- Is there something happening online at that time that you feel you are missing?
- What would you suggest instead?
- What should happen if we agree a rule and you deliberately bypass it again?

That final question matters.

Children should have a voice in a boundary. They do not need a veto over every boundary.

## Keep the consequence connected to the behaviour

If the problem is bypassing a restriction, the consequence should make sense in relation to trust and access.

For example:

- temporarily reducing flexibility around exceptions
- moving from self-managed bedtime charging to charging outside the bedroom
- requiring a parent to approve new browsers or apps for a period
- reviewing the rule together after a defined period of consistent use

Try to avoid consequences that are enormous, indefinite or unrelated.

Taking every device away for a month may feel satisfying in the moment, but it can turn the entire problem into a power struggle and leave the original issue untouched.

## Check whether the rule still fits the child

A rule that worked for an 11-year-old may feel very different at 15.

That does not mean the 15-year-old gets unrestricted access to everything. It means increasing independence should be part of the plan rather than something that happens accidentally.

A useful review asks:

**What risk is this rule protecting against?**

**Does that risk still exist?**

**Has my child shown they can manage more responsibility?**

**What would they need to demonstrate before the restriction changes?**

**What happens if trust goes backwards?**

That makes independence visible.

It also gives the child something to work towards other than "wait until you are 18".

## If the rule is about screen time, look beyond the number

Parents understandably want a clean answer to "how many hours is too much?"

The evidence is more complicated than that.

In June 2026, England's Department for Education opened a [call for evidence on screen use among children aged 5 to 16](https://www.gov.uk/government/calls-for-evidence/screen-use-by-children-aged-5-to-16), specifically asking researchers to consider differences in age, usage patterns and types of activity across health, development, wellbeing, behaviour and learning.

That is a useful reminder.

Two hours of a child video-calling grandparents, creating music and finishing school research is not identical to two hours of scrolling until 1 AM.

When deciding whether a boundary still makes sense, also ask what the screen is displacing:

- sleep
- schoolwork
- movement
- family responsibilities
- offline friendships
- meals
- other interests
- the ability to stop without the evening falling apart

The number still matters. It is just not the whole story.

## When stronger supervision may be justified

There are situations where this is more than ordinary boundary-testing.

Pay closer attention if the workaround is connected with:

- contact with an unknown or concerning person
- sexual content or requests for images
- threats, coercion or blackmail
- serious bullying
- unsafe spending
- repeated overnight use that is significantly affecting sleep or functioning
- a pattern of behaviour that is causing significant distress or impairment

In those situations, the question is no longer simply "How do I make Screen Time harder to bypass?"

The level of supervision should match the level of risk, and safeguarding or professional support may be appropriate.

## What are we trying to build?

Imagine the restriction works perfectly.

Your child cannot get around it.

That solves tonight.

It does not automatically solve 17, 18 or 21, when they own the device, know the password and decide when to stop.

So alongside the boundary, build the skill underneath it:

- recognising when enough is enough
- keeping sleep protected
- tolerating missing out
- following an agreement even when the device would let them break it
- asking for an exception instead of quietly taking one
- understanding why a family rule exists
- proving they can handle more freedom

That is the longer game.

**Protect when protection is needed. Guide while they are learning. Gradually hand the judgement back.**
