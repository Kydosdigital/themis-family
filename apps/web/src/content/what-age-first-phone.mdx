A first phone looks like one decision.

In reality, it is a bundle of decisions arriving in the same box.

Calling. Messaging. A camera. A browser. Games. App downloads. Payments. Group chats. Social media. Location sharing. Notifications. Access late at night.

So when a parent asks:

**"What age should my child get a phone?"**

the more useful question is:

**"What does my child need to be able to do, and which parts of a smartphone are they ready to manage?"**

## There is no perfect birthday

The American Academy of Pediatrics is clear on this point.

Its current guidance says research does **not** point to one perfect age when a child becomes ready for a smartphone.

The AAP recommends considering things such as:

- responsibility
- digital literacy
- honesty with caregivers
- ability to handle social conflict
- how well the child follows existing technology rules
- the practical reason the family needs the device
- whether the parent is ready to support the transition

That means two children of the same age may reasonably get different answers.

## Start with the need, not the phone

Why is the device being considered?

Common reasons include:

- walking to or from school independently
- contacting separated parents or caregivers
- travelling to activities
- staying in touch as independence increases
- coordinating pickups
- communicating with friends

Those needs may be real.

But a child who needs to call you after football practice does not automatically need public social-media accounts, unrestricted browser access and the ability to download any app.

Separate the need from the capabilities.

A family can introduce:

**Stage 1:** calls and family messages.

**Stage 2:** approved contacts, maps, camera and selected apps.

**Stage 3:** broader messaging and group chats.

**Stage 4:** selected social or creative platforms.

**Stage 5:** increasingly independent management.

The stages do not need those exact names.

The important idea is that a phone is not all-or-nothing.

## Readiness signs matter more than pleading

Most children can build a persuasive case for why everyone else already has one.

That tells you they want a phone.

It does not tell you whether they are ready.

Look at what happens in the parts of life where responsibility already exists.

Can your child:

- get schoolwork done with reasonable support?
- keep track of belongings?
- tell you when they have made a mistake?
- follow an existing device rule?
- handle "not yet" without turning every answer into a campaign?
- recognise when a friend is pressuring them?
- come to you when something uncomfortable happens?
- stop an activity when they have agreed to stop?

None of these need to be perfect.

You are looking for a pattern.

## What does recent research say?

This is an area where parents often encounter headlines that sound more certain than the evidence.

The AAP's [2026 Digital Ecosystems policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-Policy) says there is no specific age of smartphone readiness and recommends considering the individual teen's skills and needs.

AAP's [first-phone guidance](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/appropriate-age-to-introduce-a-mobile-device/) also notes that alternatives such as a basic phone or connected watch may meet a family's communication needs without immediately providing full smartphone access.

There is also emerging research examining age of acquisition and health outcomes. An [AAP News summary published in September 2026](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/35763/Teenagers-who-got-their-first-phone-at-13-were) reported an observational study in which smartphone acquisition at age 13 was associated with insufficient sleep at age 14.

That should not be translated into:

**"13 is dangerous."**

Association does not prove that receiving a phone caused the outcome, and children use phones in very different ways.

The more defensible conclusion is that **how a first phone is introduced matters**.

## Getting a phone does not mean getting social media

This is one of the easiest mistakes to avoid.

A phone is a device.

Social media is a separate environment.

Your child can have:

- calls
- texts
- maps
- camera
- family video chat
- school apps

without immediately joining every platform their friends use.

That separation gives you time to see how they handle basic communication before adding environments built around public posting, followers, recommendations, strangers and social comparison.

## Have the difficult conversations before the device arrives

The worst time to invent a phone policy is after the first problem.

Before handover, talk about:

### Downloads

Who can install apps? What makes an app okay?

### Purchases

Can the child spend money? Are in-app purchases blocked?

### Contacts

Who can message or call? What should happen if an unknown person contacts them?

### Photos

Which images should never be shared? What about photos of other people?

### Location

Which apps can access it? Who can see it?

### Bedtime

Where does the phone charge?

### School

When should it stay away?

### Mistakes

Can the child tell you something has gone wrong without assuming the first response will be "you've lost your phone forever"?

That last question matters.

A child who expects automatic punishment has a reason to hide the moment when you most need them to speak.

## Make the first phone smaller than the final phone

You do not need to hand over every feature on day one.

The [AAP's March 2026 first-phone guidance](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/getting-your-first-phone/) recommends shared family controls over downloads, internet access, contacts, content and purchases, with a smaller initial set of functions where appropriate.

That is a sensible way to think about it.

The child gets a phone.

They do not yet get an unrestricted internet passport.

## What if we already gave too much access?

You are allowed to change the setup.

Try not to frame it as:

> "We made a mistake trusting you."

Try:

> "We gave you a lot at once. Some parts are working well and some are harder than we expected. We are going to simplify the phone for a while and build things back as we get the routines right."

That keeps the focus on capability.

## Make greater freedom visible

A first-phone agreement should explain not only what is restricted, but how restrictions change.

For example:

> "For the first month, new apps need approval and the phone charges downstairs. If you're following the agreement and coming to us when something goes wrong, we'll review what you can manage independently."

A child who can see the path to more autonomy has a different relationship with the rule from a child who hears only "because you're too young".

## The first phone is practice for the phone you will not control

Eventually, your child will choose their own apps.

They will know their own passwords.

They will decide when to stop.

So the first phone is not only a safety problem to solve.

It is a training environment.

Teach:

- how to recognise manipulation
- how to leave an uncomfortable group chat
- when not to reply
- what private information means
- how to protect sleep
- how to ask before downloading
- how to tell an adult about a mistake
- how to earn more freedom

That is what readiness ultimately needs to become.
