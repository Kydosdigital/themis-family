Your child finishes an essay in twenty minutes that would normally take two hours.

You ask how.

"ChatGPT helped me."

And now you are standing in a very new kind of parenting problem. Do you congratulate them for using a modern tool? Tell them it is cheating? Ban it? Email the school?

The first thing worth knowing is this:

**"They used AI" does not tell you what they actually did with it.**

That is the question that matters.

## AI is already in the homework routine

This is not a niche issue.

[Pew Research Center reported in February 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/) that just over half of US teens had used chatbots for schoolwork. One in ten said chatbots helped with all or most of their schoolwork.

A later [Common Sense Media survey published in August 2026](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/teens-in-the-ai-era-schoolwork-and-skills-that-matter) found that 70% of surveyed US teens said they used AI for schoolwork. Among teens using AI, the ways they described using it ranged from getting direct answers to improving answers with their own knowledge.

The exact numbers should not be applied to every country. They come from US samples.

But the parenting question travels well:

**Is AI supporting the thinking, or replacing it?**

## Four very different ways to "use ChatGPT"

Imagine the homework is: "Explain why the Treaty of Versailles contributed to tensions after the First World War."

A child could ask AI:

**1. "Explain the Treaty of Versailles to me like I am 13."**

That may help them understand something they were stuck on.

**2. "Ask me five questions that will help me plan my answer."**

That may support their thinking without producing the answer.

**3. "Read my paragraph and tell me which claim needs more evidence."**

That can function like feedback.

**4. "Write me a 700-word answer at GCSE level and make it sound like a 15-year-old."**

That is doing something fundamentally different.

The technology is the same.

The cognitive work is not.

That distinction is more useful than a household rule that simply says "AI is cheating".

## School rules still matter

Even when a use seems educationally sensible, the school may have its own policy.

A teacher may want a student to complete a task independently because the struggle itself is part of the learning. An assessment may prohibit AI assistance. Another piece of work may explicitly invite students to use AI and critique its output.

So one of the first family rules should be:

**If school says a task must be done without AI, that rule comes first.**

Parents do not need to become AI-detection experts.

Ask the child to understand the rules for the work they are submitting.

## The Themis four-step AI homework check

A simple framework is:

### Explain

Can your child explain what the task is asking?

If they cannot explain the question without AI, the AI is already working on top of a gap.

### Attempt

Have they done the part they are reasonably expected to try themselves?

That does not mean every child must struggle alone for an arbitrary amount of time. It means they should not automatically outsource the first moment of difficulty.

### Ask

What exactly are they asking AI to do?

"Explain this concept", "quiz me", "show me where my reasoning is weak" and "write my answer" are not equivalent requests.

### Check

Can they verify the final result and explain it in their own words?

AI can sound very confident while being wrong. A polished answer is not the same thing as an accurate one.

If your child cannot explain the work they are submitting, that is a strong sign that too much thinking may have been handed over.

## What I would say instead of "AI is banned"

Try:

> "I am not worried that you used a new tool. I want to understand what work the tool did and what work you did. Show me how you used it."

That one question gives you far more information.

Then ask:

- What did you know before you opened the chatbot?
- What did you ask it?
- Which parts of the answer did you check?
- Did you use any sources outside the chatbot?
- Could you explain this to me without reopening the conversation?
- Does your school allow this kind of help?

You are teaching a process, not conducting a police interview.

## "But they will just hide it"

Possibly.

Children have hidden calculators, answer sheets, copied homework and messages long before generative AI existed.

The answer cannot be perfect surveillance.

What you can do is make the family standard clear and make the thinking visible.

For a younger child, that may mean using AI in a shared space or showing the prompt history for schoolwork.

For an older teenager, it may mean more independence with an expectation that they can explain their process and comply with school rules.

The level of oversight should change as their judgement develops.

## Teach them where AI is weak

AI literacy is not just "learning to prompt".

Children need to know that an AI response can:

- invent information
- misstate a source
- flatten a complicated issue
- sound certain when the evidence is uncertain
- reproduce bias
- give an answer that is technically plausible but contextually wrong
- produce work that does not sound remotely like them

Ofcom's [2026 Children and Parents report](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-children/childrens) shows that AI is already part of young people's media environment in the UK, with 56% of 8- to 17-year-olds saying they had used AI.

That makes critical judgement more important, not less.

The family goal should not be a child who never encounters AI.

It should be a child who knows when not to trust it.

## Do not miss the privacy conversation

Children can paste astonishing amounts of personal information into a chatbot without thinking of it as "sharing data".

A useful family rule is:

**Do not paste private information about yourself or another person into an AI tool unless you understand where that information is going and why it is needed.**

That includes:

- full names linked with private circumstances
- addresses and locations
- passwords
- school login details
- private messages
- medical information
- intimate images
- information about another child who has not agreed to share it

UNICEF's [Guidance on AI and Children](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children) puts privacy, safety, transparency, wellbeing and children's rights at the centre of child-focused AI.

Those are useful family principles too.

## What if AI has already replaced the learning?

Do not make the only consequence "no AI ever again".

First work out what has happened.

If your child has been routinely generating homework they cannot explain:

1. tell them clearly why that is a problem
2. check the school's policy
3. decide whether the school needs to be informed
4. temporarily increase supervision around homework
5. rebuild the missing skill
6. agree what AI uses are allowed going forward

The missing skill matters.

If AI wrote every paragraph for six months, the long-term solution is not only blocking ChatGPT. Your child may now need practice planning, drafting, researching or tolerating the discomfort of not knowing immediately.

That is what needs rebuilding.

## A family AI agreement can be short

You do not need 27 rules.

A useful starting agreement might be:

**We can use AI to understand, practise, brainstorm and check.**

**We do not submit AI-generated work as our own when the task expects our own work.**

**School rules come first.**

**We check important claims against reliable sources.**

**We do not share private information unnecessarily.**

**If we are unsure whether a use is okay, we ask.**

That is teachable.

It also survives the arrival of the next chatbot.

## This is bigger than cheating

There is a reason Themis talks about building, not only blocking.

AI can help a young person code something, analyse an idea, practise a language, challenge an argument, plan a project or explore a subject they became curious about at 10 PM.

The goal is not to remove that possibility.

The goal is to teach the difference between **using a tool** and **giving the tool your responsibility to think**.

That is a lesson they will need long after homework is over.
