The question sounds as if there should be a number.

Two hours. Three hours. Ninety minutes on a school night.

For younger children, some guidance does use more specific limits. But once children move into school age and adolescence, a single daily number becomes much less useful because **what they are doing, when they are doing it and what the screen is replacing all matter**.

A child can spend an hour video-calling family, an hour researching homework and an hour making music. Another child can spend three hours scrolling until after midnight.

The clock sees three hours in both cases.

The child's life does not.

## Start with what the screen is crowding out

The American Academy of Pediatrics' current approach places more emphasis on balance, content and context than on one universal number for older children and teens.

Its 2026 guidance asks families to protect the things children still need whether phones exist or not:

- enough sleep
- school and homework
- physical activity
- face-to-face relationships
- family responsibilities
- meals
- time to be bored, create, play and recover

That gives you a better first question than "How many hours?"

Ask:

**What is screen use taking time away from in my child's life?**

If the honest answer is "nothing important right now", you may be looking at a different problem from a family where gaming is pushing bedtime to 1 AM every night.

## Not all screen time is the same

Parents often need a household rule that is simple enough to use.

That does not mean every minute should be treated identically.

A practical family plan can separate:

**Necessary use:** school platforms, research, travel, family communication.

**Constructive use:** coding, drawing, making music, writing, editing video, learning a skill.

**Social use:** messaging friends, group chats, video calls, multiplayer games.

**Entertainment use:** scrolling, watching videos, gaming, browsing.

The categories overlap. A multiplayer game can also be a child's main social space. A YouTube video can be either entertainment or a guitar lesson.

The point is not to create a spreadsheet for every minute.

It is to stop the phrase "screen time" from hiding what is actually happening.

## What does the research say?

The relationship between screen use and wellbeing is not one simple cause-and-effect story.

The [American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 policy on children's digital ecosystems](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-Policy) focuses on the design of digital environments, the child's developmental stage and whether media is displacing healthy activities.

Sleep is one area where the evidence is more consistent.

A [2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41770550/) found a small association within the same young person between higher daily screen use and later sleep onset. A separate [2025 umbrella review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41154921/) found overall negative associations between digital-device use and adolescent sleep, particularly around late-night use, social media and internet use.

Those findings still do not create one perfect daily allowance.

They tell us that **timing and displacement matter**.

## A useful screen-time audit

Before reducing the number, spend three days noticing the pattern.

You are looking for five things.

### 1. Sleep

Is the device delaying bedtime, waking your child through notifications, or making mornings harder?

### 2. Responsibilities

Are homework, chores, getting ready and family commitments repeatedly losing to the screen?

### 3. Movement

Does your child still move their body most days and have opportunities to be outdoors or active?

### 4. Relationships

Are they still talking, laughing, arguing, visiting, eating and doing ordinary life with other people?

### 5. Ability to stop

Can your child put the device down when required without every transition turning into a major conflict?

One problem area does not automatically mean "too much screen time".

It tells you where the rule may need to be more specific.

## Replace vague limits with protected times

"Less screen time" is difficult to enforce.

"Phones charge downstairs at 9:30" is clear.

"No gaming until homework and tomorrow's bag are done" is clear.

"No phones at the table" is clear.

"One episode, then shower and bed" is clear.

This is one reason Themis focuses on **rules that describe what happens and when**, rather than only showing parents a total number of minutes.

A family might protect:

- breakfast
- homework time
- dinner
- one evening activity
- the last hour before sleep
- overnight
- particular family events

Then recreational screen use can happen around those anchors.

That can feel less like the entire day is a negotiation.

## What if my child says everyone else gets more?

They may be right.

Different families will set different limits.

You do not need to prove that your rule is statistically normal.

Explain what the rule is protecting.

> "This isn't because screens are bad. We have noticed you are struggling to get up for school when you're online late, so sleep is the part we are protecting."

That is stronger than:

> "Because I said so and you're on that phone too much."

The first gives the child a reason they can eventually learn to manage themselves.

## Review the rule instead of letting it become permanent by accident

A limit that was sensible at 10 should not automatically remain unchanged at 15.

Set a review point.

For example:

> "We'll try this for two weeks. If bedtime is working, schoolwork is getting done and you are stopping without us fighting every night, we can look at whether you need more flexibility."

That gives responsibility somewhere to go.

It also means the child knows that good judgement can lead to more freedom.

## When the number really does need to come down

You may need a firmer reduction when screen use is repeatedly:

- cutting into sleep
- replacing schoolwork
- stopping regular physical activity
- causing missed meals or responsibilities
- isolating your child from relationships they previously valued
- continuing despite clear negative consequences
- creating serious conflict almost every time it ends

Even then, do not only remove time.

Work out what the screen has been doing for your child.

Is it connection? Escape? Competition? Status? Comfort? Boredom relief?

If you remove three hours of gaming from a child whose whole friendship group meets there, you have removed more than three hours of entertainment.

That does not mean the limit is wrong.

It means the replacement matters.

## A better family question

Instead of:

**"What is the correct amount of screen time?"**

Try:

**"Does technology still fit inside a healthy life for this child?"**

That gives you something you can actually observe.

And it gives your child a skill they will eventually need to use without you.
