An agreement does not need to predict every evening. A friend’s birthday, an unfinished conversation or a teacher’s video can change what is reasonable today.

Planning how to ask for an exception can be as useful as setting the original rule.

## Be specific about the request

“Can I have my phone back?” leaves a lot unsaid. “Can I use YouTube for twenty minutes to watch the video for tomorrow’s lesson?” gives the adult a clearer decision.

That does not mean every request needs a long explanation. A short reason and a specific amount of time can be enough.

## A different answer can still be an answer

A parent might agree to ten minutes rather than twenty. They might need one clarification or decide that the original agreement still stands.

The useful part is making that outcome understandable. An unanswered request should not look like an approval.

## Give temporary access an end

If access is meant to last twenty minutes, make that visible. Otherwise, somebody has to remember to bring the conversation up again.

Themis’s temporary grants are designed with an explicit target and expiry. Their real-device behaviour is part of the product’s verification work before launch.

## School access has limits

An app can contain both useful school material and entertainment. Themis cannot tell which YouTube video a child intends to watch.

A temporary grant gives access to the selected app or site. That limitation belongs in the conversation, rather than being hidden behind a label such as “school mode”.

## Keep the discussion human

A request tool can carry a short explanation. It should not try to become the place for every difficult family conversation.

Sometimes the next step is simply to talk.
