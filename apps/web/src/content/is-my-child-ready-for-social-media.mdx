Your child has reached the platform's minimum age.

That answers one question.

It does not answer:

**Are they ready?**

A birthday cannot tell you whether a young person can handle exclusion, a cruel comment, a stranger in their messages, an algorithm repeatedly serving upsetting content, a friend posting a private screenshot or the pressure to keep checking what everybody else is doing.

Social-media readiness is a skills question as much as an age question.

## Social media is not automatically good or bad

The American Psychological Association's health advisory makes an important point:

Social media is **not inherently beneficial or harmful** to young people.

Its effects depend on the child, their circumstances, the content they encounter and the features of the platform.

For one teenager, a platform may provide friendship, identity, creative expression or access to a community they do not have offline.

For another, the same platform may amplify comparison, sleep loss, conflict or harmful content.

That is why "social media is bad" is not a useful readiness test.

## Six things I would look for before saying yes

### 1. They understand that the audience can change

A post intended for friends can be screenshotted, forwarded or copied.

Does your child understand that "private account" does not mean "content can never leave this group"?

### 2. They can handle ordinary peer conflict

Social media does not create every friendship problem.

It can make one disagreement visible, permanent and available at midnight.

How does your child cope when somebody leaves them out, disagrees with them or says something unkind?

### 3. They can stop

Can they put an existing device away for dinner, homework or sleep?

A platform built around endless content is a difficult place to learn the very first version of self-control.

### 4. They can recognise unsafe contact

Do they know what to do if someone they do not know:

- asks personal questions
- gives intense compliments
- wants to move to private messaging
- asks for photos
- tells them to keep the relationship secret
- offers gifts
- asks to meet

### 5. They can ask for help

This may be the most important one.

Your child does not need to know how to solve every online problem.

They need to be able to say:

> "Something weird happened."

without believing that telling you will automatically end their social life.

### 6. You are ready too

Are you prepared to:

- learn the platform
- set it up with them
- check privacy and contact settings
- discuss what appears in their feed
- respond without panic when they make a mistake
- increase freedom when they demonstrate good judgement

Social-media readiness is partly a family readiness question.

## What does the research say about age?

The [American Psychological Association's advisory](https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use) says there is no evidence that young people become unaffected by social-media risks at one specific age.

Development is gradual.

The APA notes that early adolescence, roughly ages 10 to 14, is a period of major biological, social and psychological change. It advises ongoing adult review, discussion and coaching around social-media content for most young people in this period, while balancing that oversight with appropriate privacy.

That does not mean every 14-year-old is ready.

It also does not mean a 15-year-old should be monitored exactly like an 11-year-old.

Age matters.

It just does not do all the work.

## Start with one platform, not the whole internet

A child asking for social media may mean:

"Everyone is on WhatsApp."

"I want TikTok."

"My team uses Snapchat."

"I want to post my drawings on Instagram."

Those are different environments.

Do not bundle them into one yes.

Choose one platform and learn it together.

Look at:

- who can contact the account
- whether location is shared
- whether strangers can comment
- direct-message settings
- recommendation settings
- reporting tools
- time controls
- whether the account can be searched publicly

Then watch how the first platform goes before adding another.

## Your first rules should cover situations, not just minutes

A 45-minute limit does not tell a child what to do when somebody asks for a nude image.

Your agreement should include behaviour.

For example:

**We do not share passwords with friends.**

**We do not send intimate images.**

**We do not meet an online-only contact without involving a parent or trusted adult.**

**If a conversation becomes sexual, threatening or secretive, we bring in an adult.**

**Phones do not stay active through the night.**

**We do not post another person's private content without permission.**

**If something goes wrong, telling us is the first step, not automatic punishment.**

These rules teach judgement.

## What about monitoring?

Monitoring should change with age and capability.

The APA describes monitoring in early adolescence as ongoing review, discussion and coaching, not simply secret surveillance.

That distinction matters.

A [2026 systematic review involving 26,337 adolescents](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41069112/) found parental communication, modelling, monitoring and limit-setting were all relevant factors, but results for monitoring were mixed. Proactive limit-setting appeared more useful than limits introduced only after problems had already developed.

Tell your child what you can see.

Tell them why.

Tell them what you will not routinely inspect.

Tell them how that changes if a serious safety concern appears.

Transparency is part of the safety system.

## Trial the account

You do not have to decide forever on day one.

Try:

> "We'll set this up together for four weeks. We will check how sleep, school, friendships and the account itself are going. Then we decide what changes."

During the trial, ask questions that are not accusations:

- What's the funniest thing you've seen this week?
- Has anything made you feel left out?
- Does your feed ever show you things you don't want?
- Is there anyone messaging you who you don't actually know?
- What happens in your group when somebody takes a screenshot?
- Does the app make it hard to stop?

You are learning the environment through your child.

## Watch what the platform is doing to the child

Do not wait for a dramatic crisis.

Look for changes.

Is your child:

- repeatedly losing sleep
- becoming distressed about appearance or popularity
- unable to stop despite wanting to
- lying to maintain access
- withdrawing from offline relationships
- being pulled toward self-harm, eating-disorder or other dangerous content
- receiving unwanted sexual contact
- increasingly anxious after using the app

The APA advises screening for signs of problematic social-media use when it begins interfering with ordinary roles and routines.

That is a better trigger for intervention than simply noticing the weekly screen-time report is high.

## Readiness is not the end of parenting

Giving permission does not mean stepping away.

The first months of social media should involve more conversation, not less.

And the goal is not to create a young person who needs you to approve every post forever.

It is to build someone who can ask:

**Should I share this?**

**Who can see it?**

**Why is this account asking me that?**

**What is this content doing to how I feel?**

**Do I need to leave this conversation?**

**Should I tell someone?**

That is the independence Themis is ultimately trying to support.
