Your child says they have a new friend.

They met in a game.

Or Discord.

Or a group chat.

Or somebody followed them, replied to a story and started talking.

You ask who the person is.

Your child says:

> "I don't know them in real life, but they're nice."

That sentence can make a parent want to shut everything down immediately.

Sometimes an unknown online contact really is dangerous.

But the safest next step is not to panic at the word "stranger".

It is to understand the relationship.

## Online does not automatically mean fake, and friendly does not automatically mean safe

Young people build real friendships online.

They play games together, share interests, study, create things and talk to people they may never meet offline.

So a family rule of:

**"Never speak to anyone you don't already know in real life"**

may sound simple, but it can be difficult to apply in the online environments children actually use.

A better safety question is:

**What is this person asking my child to do, share, hide or believe?**

## First, find out what "talking" means

Do not assume.

Ask:

- Where did you meet?
- How long have you been talking?
- What do you know about them?
- Do they say they are your age?
- Have you video-called?
- Have they asked where you live or go to school?
- Have they asked for photos?
- Have they sent gifts, money or game items?
- Have they asked you to move to another app?
- Have they told you not to tell me?
- Have they talked about sex?
- Have they asked to meet?

The answers matter more than the label "online friend".

## What grooming can look like

The [NSPCC defines grooming](https://www.nspcc.org.uk/keeping-children-safe/types-of-abuse/grooming/) as someone building a relationship, trust and emotional connection with a child so they can manipulate, exploit or abuse them.

It can happen online, offline or across both.

Groomers may:

- pretend to be younger
- learn the child's interests
- give intense attention
- offer gifts or game currency
- act like a mentor or romantic partner
- encourage secrecy
- gradually sexualise conversation
- isolate the child from trusted people
- move communication into private channels
- use guilt, threats or blackmail
- ask for sexual images
- push for an in-person meeting

A [2026 systematic review of offender strategies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41618489/) found that online sexual-grooming strategies are highly adaptive and individualised rather than following one identical script.

That is why teaching children a single stereotype of "a creepy old man pretending to be 14" is not enough.

## Do not make your child's embarrassment work against you

If you react with:

> "How could you be so stupid?"

you have created a new problem.

A child can be manipulated precisely because the other person worked to make the relationship feel real.

They may feel loyalty, affection, fear, shame or confusion.

They may also worry that if they tell you the truth, you will remove every device and every friendship.

UNICEF's [guidance on responding to negative online experiences](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-care/supporting-child-negative-online-experience) recommends staying calm and helping the child feel heard and supported, because that makes future disclosure more likely.

Calm does not mean passive.

You can take serious action without making the child the person on trial.

## Separate ordinary online friendship from red flags

An online contact becomes more concerning when there is:

**Secrecy**

"Don't tell your parents about me."

**Identity manipulation**

Stories that do not add up, refusal to verify basic information, or pretending to be a peer.

**Isolation**

"Your family doesn't understand you like I do."

**Escalation**

Moving quickly from public game chat to private encrypted messaging.

**Gifts**

Unexpected money, devices, game items or other rewards tied to the relationship.

**Sexualisation**

Questions about the child's body, relationships, sexual experience or requests for sexual content.

**Pressure**

Demanding fast replies, guilt-tripping, threatening or becoming controlling.

**Image requests**

Especially nude or sexual images.

**Meeting requests**

Particularly plans that exclude parents or another trusted adult.

One red flag does not always tell you the full story.

Several together should change how urgently you act.

## If the contact seems ordinary

Use it as a teaching moment.

You can say:

> "I'm not assuming this person is dangerous. I want us to use the same safety rules with anyone we only know online."

Agree rules such as:

- do not share home address or school details
- keep location sharing off
- do not send intimate images
- do not send money
- do not keep a relationship secret from trusted adults
- do not move to a private platform because someone pressures you
- do not arrange an in-person meeting without involving a parent or trusted adult
- block and tell someone if the contact becomes sexual, threatening or manipulative

UNICEF's [online privacy checklist](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-care/online-privacy) also recommends helping children understand that not everyone they meet online is trustworthy and reviewing privacy and contact settings.

## If you think grooming may be happening

Shift from general digital parenting into safeguarding.

Do not ask your child to continue talking to the person just so you can collect more evidence.

Do not impersonate your child to set a trap.

Do not confront a suspected abuser in a way that could increase risk or destroy evidence.

Instead:

- stop or restrict the unsafe contact
- preserve relevant messages, usernames and account details where safe
- use the platform's reporting tools
- seek child-protection or law-enforcement guidance appropriate to your country
- get professional support for the child if needed

In the UK, CEOP and the NSPCC provide specific reporting and support routes. In other countries, use the relevant national child-protection or police service.

If a child is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

## What if sexual images have already been sent?

Do not shame your child.

The urgent questions are:

- Who has the image?
- Is someone threatening to share it?
- Is money being demanded?
- Is the child being pressured to send more?
- Is an adult involved?
- Is the image being circulated?

This may be sexual coercion, sextortion or child sexual exploitation.

Get appropriate safeguarding help.

Do not casually forward the image to yourself or other people. Laws and reporting procedures around sexual images of minors vary by jurisdiction, and preserving evidence should be done carefully.

## Should I take the phone away?

Sometimes temporary restrictions are needed to stop contact.

But think carefully before making "you told me, therefore you lose your phone" the automatic sequence.

A child who loses every connection after disclosing a worrying contact may learn:

**Next time, hide it.**

You can say:

> "We may need to change some settings while we sort this out. That is to stop the unsafe contact, not because you are being punished for telling me."

That distinction matters.

## Make unknown-contact rules age appropriate

For a younger child, you may decide:

- only approved contacts can communicate
- multiplayer chat is off
- new contacts need parent approval
- games happen in shared areas

For an older teenager, the rule may be:

- you can meet people online
- private information stays private
- sexual or secretive contact gets brought to an adult
- offline meetings involve a trusted adult and public location
- parents step in if specific safety concerns appear

The protection should evolve as capability develops.

## The skill we are really teaching

You cannot pre-approve every human your child will ever encounter online.

Eventually, they need to notice patterns themselves:

**Why does this person need this to be secret?**

**Why are they asking me to move apps?**

**Why are they giving me things?**

**Why are they asking for a photo I would not show my family?**

**Why do I feel scared to stop replying?**

**Who can I tell?**

That is why Themis treats protection and education as the same journey.

A technical boundary can stop some contact today.

Judgement is what protects the young person when the boundary is no longer there.
